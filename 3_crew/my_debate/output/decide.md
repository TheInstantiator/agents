The side arguing in favor of going for the touchdown (the "pro" side) presents the more convincing argument. Their case effectively balances the high reward of seven points against the three from a field goal, while asserting that the success probability for a 1-yard touchdown attempt is comparable to or exceeds that of a field goal when factoring in variables like snap, hold, and kick reliability. This is a strong point, as it directly challenges the perceived safety of the field goal without dismissing it outright. Moreover, they smartly address the downside of failure by noting that it pins the opponent deep in their own territory at the 1-yard line, turning a potential loss into an immediate defensive advantage and preserving field position momentum. This holistic view—maximizing upside while minimizing effective risk—makes their strategy feel proactive and game-altering.

In contrast, the side advocating for the field goal (the "con" side) relies heavily on the near-certainty of three points from a short 19-yard kick, which is a valid emphasis on prudence. However, they overstate the unreliability of the touchdown attempt by focusing on the "concentrated goal-line defense" without providing evidence that its success rate is meaningfully lower than the field goal's, especially since the pro side claims equivalence. Their argument also undervalues the field position benefit after a failed attempt, framing failure solely as "zero points and a turnover" that "squanders" the opportunity, which ignores how that deep pinning can lead to further scoring chances or force opponent errors. While securing guaranteed points is appealing, the con side's calculus feels overly conservative and doesn't fully counter the pro's integrated risk-reward analysis, making it less persuasive overall. Thus, the pro side wins the debate on the merits of superior strategic depth and comprehensive reasoning.
